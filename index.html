What this radar is

A quick overview, not a forecast of its own. Flying Radar is built to read the models the way an experienced pilot reads their own tools (XC Skies, Skysight, the NWS discussion and the rest) and boil that daily routine down to one page. Instead of clicking through everything multiple times every day, you get a glance at which day and which site are worth keeping an eye on. It is not trying to be better than XC Skies, Skysight or any other prediction; it is designed to be faster to read. It does not replace your own weather research before a flight, a look at the sky, the wind on launch or your own judgement: a guide, not a launch decision.

What you see. The Weather Overview sums up the pattern for the next day or two (tap for the NWS discussion in short). Each day card shows the day type (Front, Pre-frontal, Post-frontal, Santa Ana, Switch day), a rain pill when it's likely wet, and the sites rated 3 or better, best first. Open a day for all sites; open a site for height, winds and rain. Today keeps updating all day; the Archive (bottom of the page) keeps each day as it was forecast around midday (the last run between noon and 3 pm), and every past day is kept there (bottom of the page) with any flights logged for it.

How it's tuned. By hand, against the other tools rather than against flights: the rules are adjusted until the radar reads a day the way XC Skies, Skysight, the NWS discussion and experienced local pilots read the same forecast. Little Black, Blossom, Elsinore and Torrey are calibrated this way; Otay, Soboba, Marshall, Santa Barbara and Laguna/Horse/Fuzz are still educated guesses. Weakest spots: sea-breeze timing, convergence lines and scattered showers.

How the rating works
1 no go 2 marginal 3 good local / glass-off 4 XC 5 outstanding

Optimistic by design. The rating says how good a day can be from the numbers: it takes the best hours and leaves the judgement calls to you. So it will more often be better than the day turns out than worse.

How a score is made. The models give each site a number from the height of lift and the thermal strength in the site's window, on the site's own height scale (Little Black: 1,800 ft a good local day, 2,300 ft XC starts, 3,500 ft great). Then the same deductions apply at every site:

A 5needs the site's great height, the wind blowing into launch, and a clean day: any deduction makes it a 4 at most

Rain and cloud together take off at most 2.5. Torrey is a ridge: showers and cloud don't count on a ridge day (land when a cell comes), over 20 mph or gusts over 25 is too windy, straight south or north is a no-go; on a thermal day the full deductions apply. Shown but not in the score: the day type, the direction of the wind aloft (for planning an XC), model agreement and the chance of flying or XC.

The scale. 1 no go, 2 marginal, 3 a good local day or a proper glass-off, 4 a real XC window, 5 outstanding.

Height first. For every hour the radar works out the top of usable lift from each model's temperature and wind profile: how high a dry thermal rises in the mixed layer, only the part where climbs beat a glider's sink rate (RASP's Hcrit idea), never above cloudbase. This is the same idea, and close to the same numbers, as XC Skies (checked at the 56 bend on Oct 26 at noon: HRRR 1,978 vs 1,968 ft, RRFS 1,649 vs 1,640 ft). Each site has its own height scale for "good local", "XC starts" and "great" (Little Black: 1,800 / 2,300 / 3,500 ft), and an hour also needs a usable launch wind (gusts over 12 mph cost half a point for the day, over 18 mph is too windy; at Torrey over 20 mph or gusts over 25 is too windy), wind aloft that isn't too strong, and no rain. Most sites also read the lift at points around the launch (10 km out, plus a few hand-picked ones) and average the four best readings, at most two per point.

Site rules. Little Black and Otay are rated in the 11 am–1 pm window and Blossom 11 am–2 pm, on the average of the two best hours (a strong hour counts, a one-hour model spike only half); glass-off is rated separately and the better of the two counts. Blossom's height is the best of its launch and the El Cajon Mountain peak. Elsinore needs an easterly or light morning launch wind, then the west switch inside the window (west all morning is a 1); its height is the best top of lift along the ridge and the Inland Empire toward Soboba, never over the lake. Torrey needs a westerly: SSW–SW and NW–NNW count as cross, and straight south or north (from about 205° and 330° outward) is a no-go. It is 2–3 for ridge soaring, 4 on a great ridge day (WSW to WNW, 240–300°, either side of west equally good, reaching 10+ mph and holding 7+ through the afternoon; the models usually run light on the sea breeze) and 5 on a thermal day. Laguna/Horse/Fuzz is XC-only (2 at most without a convergence setup). Whale's V (non-permitted, coordinate with the DZ) is rated 10 am to sunset, SW–W only and a no-go in any north wind; 4 when lift reaches 3,000 ft. Marshall, Santa Barbara and Soboba use their own windows; Santa Barbara's lift is worked out from launch height, above the marine layer.

Models. While HRRR covers a day, HRRR ×2 and RRFS ×1 decide; then RRFS ×2 plus the global models (GFS, ECMWF, ICON together counting once); after that the global models alone. RRFS runs low, so its heights are raised by its recent gap to HRRR before scoring (the heights shown are the model's own). The ensembles give the chance of a flyable or XC day. The models behind each day are listed at the bottom right of its card.

Day type. The day type comes from the models over the region (rain, cooling and the 850/700 hPa winds); a percentage on it means the models disagree, and Pre/post-frontal means between two fronts or the models split. The rain pill on a day shows when half the runs are wet across the San Diego sites.

High cloud. Mid and high cloud only (cirrus, alto-; not cumulus or the marine layer), averaged over each site and four points about 20 km around it, from HRRR and RRFS (ECMWF and ICON further out), 10 am to sunset.
