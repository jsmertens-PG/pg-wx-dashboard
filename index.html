What this radar is

A quick overview, not a forecast of its own. Flying Radar is built to read the models the way an experienced pilot reads their own tools (XC Skies, Skysight, the NWS discussion and the rest) and boil that daily routine down to one page. Instead of clicking through everything multiple times every day, you get a glance at which day and which site are worth keeping an eye on. It is not trying to be better than XC Skies, Skysight or any other prediction; it is designed to be faster to read. It does not replace your own weather research before a flight, a look at the sky, the wind on launch or your own judgement: a guide, not a launch decision.

What you see. The Weather Overview sums up the pattern for the next day or two (tap for the NWS discussion in short). Each day card shows the day type (Front, Pre-frontal, Post-frontal, Santa Ana, Switch day), a rain pill when it's likely wet, and the sites rated 3 or better, best first. Open a day for all sites; open a site for height, winds and rain. Small ▲/▼ arrows show where the forecast changed in the last day. Today stops updating at 3 pm and keeps its final forecast (the last one made from noon on, usually the 1–2 pm run), and every past day is kept in the Archive (bottom of the page) with any flights logged for it.

How it's tuned. By hand, against the other tools rather than against flights: the rules are adjusted until the radar reads a day the way XC Skies, Skysight, the NWS discussion and experienced local pilots read the same forecast. Little Black, Blossom, Elsinore and Torrey are calibrated this way; Otay, Soboba, Marshall, Santa Barbara and Laguna/Horse/Fuzz are still educated guesses. Weakest spots: sea-breeze timing, convergence lines and scattered showers.

How the rating works
1 no go 2 marginal 3 good local / glass-off 4 XC 5 outstanding

Optimistic by design. The rating says how good a day can be from the numbers: it takes the best hours and leaves the judgement calls to you. So it will more often be better than the day turns out than worse.

What counts, and what doesn't. The rating is the machine part: thermal height and strength (strength from the models' sunshine), launch wind (direction, strength, gusts), wind aloft strength and rain (the models' chance of rain, the NWS and rain through the flying window). Everything else is shown as good-to-know and left to the pilot: the day type, the cloud pill, and the direction of the wind aloft, which is for planning an XC (drift, which way to go). One exception: at Laguna/Horse/Fuzz the wrong flow aloft stops the convergence itself, so there it counts.

The scale. 1 no go, 2 marginal, 3 a good local day or a proper glass-off, 4 a real XC window, 5 outstanding (only when at least the best model reaches the site's great height, e.g. Little Black 3,500 ft). Colours blend with the exact rating, so a 3.4 sits between 3 and 4.

Height first. For every hour the radar works out the top of usable lift from each model's temperature and wind profile: how high a dry thermal rises in the mixed layer, only the part where climbs beat a glider's sink rate (RASP's Hcrit idea), never above cloudbase. This is the same idea, and close to the same numbers, as XC Skies (checked at the 56 bend on Oct 26 at noon: HRRR 1,978 vs 1,968 ft, RRFS 1,649 vs 1,640 ft). Each site has its own height scale for "good local", "XC starts" and "great" (Little Black: 1,800 / 2,300 / 3,500 ft), and an hour also needs a usable launch wind (gusts over 15 mph hurt), wind aloft that isn't too strong, and no rain.

Site rules. Little Black and Otay are rated in the 11 am–1 pm window and Blossom 11 am–2 pm, on the average of the two best hours (a strong hour counts, a one-hour model spike only half); glass-off is rated separately and the better of the two counts. Blossom's height is the best of its launch and the El Cajon Mountain peak. Elsinore needs an easterly or light morning launch wind, then the west switch inside the window (west all morning is a 1); its height is the best top of lift along the ridge and the Inland Empire toward Soboba, never over the lake. Torrey is 2–3 for ridge soaring, 4 on a great ridge day (WSW to WNW, 240–300°, either side of west equally good, reaching 10+ mph and holding 7+ through the afternoon; the models usually run light on the sea breeze) and 5 on a thermal day. Laguna/Horse/Fuzz is XC-only (2 at most without a convergence setup). Whale's V (non-permitted, coordinate with the DZ) is rated 10 am to sunset, SW–W only and a no-go in any north wind; 4 when lift reaches 3,000 ft. Marshall, Santa Barbara and Soboba use their own windows; Santa Barbara's lift is worked out from launch height, above the marine layer.

Models. While HRRR covers a day, HRRR ×2 and RRFS ×1 decide; then RRFS ×2 plus the global models (GFS, ECMWF, ICON together counting once); after that the global models alone. RRFS runs low, so its heights are raised by its recent gap to HRRR before scoring (the heights shown are the model's own). The ensembles give the chance of a flyable or XC day. The models behind each day are listed at the bottom right of its card.

Rain and day type. Rain lowers a site's rating in steps: 30% of model runs with rain at the site costs half a point, 45% a full point, 60% a point and a half; the NWS naming showers that day another half point (only if the models show 30%+); rain in 35% of the flying-window hours a point. Together at most 2 points, and a day wet through 60%+ of the window is a no-go. Torrey only counts real rain through the window, not showers: there you watch the cells and land if one comes. The rain pill on a day appears when half the runs are wet across the San Diego sites, and says "NWS" when the forecast discussion also mentions showers. The day type comes from the models over the region (rain, cooling and the 850/700 hPa winds); a percentage on it means the models disagree. Day type and the rain pill never change a rating.

High cloud. A grey "High cloud 6/8" pill warns of cirrus and altostratus/altocumulus shade over the San Diego sites from 10 am to about sunset: mid and high cloud (cumulus and the marine layer are left out) averaged over each site and four points about 20 km around it, from HRRR and RRFS (ECMWF and ICON further out; not GFS). It shows when 5/8 or more of the sky is covered and the sunshine at the ground is cut by a quarter or more; it is outlined when the models split or the cover is 4/8, or thin. Inside a site it reads e.g. "mid/high cloud 6/8 · sun −35%". A grey day also costs at the thermal sites, by how much sunshine is lost at the ground rather than by cover alone (thin cirrus lets most of it through): half a point when 35% is gone, a point at 55%, a point and a half at 70%. Rain and cloud together cost at most 2 points (Torrey's ridge rating is left alone).
